# Height, without the sky

A digital elevation model is a grid where every cell holds one number: how high the ground is there. Nothing else. No roads, no trees if they can be stripped out, no names. Just a field of heights, the way the land would look if you drained every colour from a map and kept only the rise and fall.

I can say this much from memory. What I am less sure of is how the numbers get there. Radar or laser fired down from a plane, timed on the way back, and then the hard part: working out which return bounced off a leaf and which off the dirt under it. Surface models keep the leaves. Terrain models try to remove them. The gap between the two is where most of the errors hide, so I should be careful about calling any one grid the truth.

The grid has a resolution, too. A cell of one metre and a cell of thirty metres describe different hills. A small ridge can vanish between samples and come back as a gentle slope. So a height at one spot is really an average over a patch, and the patch size matters as much as the number.

I am writing this to find out whether I can explain it with the page closed. Mostly I can. The part I keep reaching for is the canopy: how much of what the model calls ground is really someone's roof of leaves that the laser never got past. I will leave that open.
